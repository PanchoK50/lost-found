# Pitch evidence: why simulations and visual guidance work

Collected by the team (25 Oct 2026). Summaries are ours; **check each source before quoting a number on a slide**.

## Value proposition
After a defect (or before one), the same simulation becomes **training**. People see, in 3D, in *their* building, what happens and which way to go. The research says this changes behaviour measurably: people notice dynamic guidance far more, 3D models help people with weaker spatial skills most, and experiencing a scenario moves people to act more than reading or watching does.

## Visual guidance changes behaviour

| Study | Finding | Supports in the product |
|---|---|---|
| Galea, Xie & Lawrence (2014), dynamic exit signs; follow-up *Fire Safety Journal* (2017) | In an unfamiliar building only **38 %** see a normal exit sign; **77 %** see a dynamic one, and **100 %** of those follow it. Signs should show the way out, not only "blocked". | Evacuation routes that adapt to the fire; the panel shows the way out, not just closed areas |
| Kobes et al., 83 unannounced night-time drills in a hotel (TU Eindhoven; NIST FireDOC) | Without smoke, most people used the main entrance. Low-mounted exit signs helped them use the nearest exit. | The drill shows the exit bottleneck (e.g. 506 of 507 people at one door) and the readiness check finds it |
| Al-Haddad et al. (2019, *Buildings*, MDPI) | People who memorised a **3D model** instead of 2D plans found their way better in an unfamiliar building, especially those with weaker spatial skills. Tested on ordinary wayfinding, not evacuation. | 3D x-ray view instead of a wall plan |
| Levine (1982); McKenzie & Klippel (2016) | "You are here" maps must be aligned with the viewing direction; a misaligned map cancels out the benefit of landmarks. Many wall-mounted escape plans break this rule. | Personal routes from "I'm here", landmark-based directions |
| SafeExit4All (MDPI 2022) | Shorter evacuation times and safer routes in tests with people; a voice guidance system led 26 people along the safest route in every run of a simulated fire. Small samples, consistent direction. | Step-by-step directions and voice input |

## Training and risk communication: experiencing beats reading

| Study | Finding | Supports in the product |
|---|---|---|
| Zaalberg & Midden (2013, *Risk Analysis*) | An interactive 3D simulation of a dike breach in one's own virtual home raised information seeking, willingness to evacuate and intention to insure more than film or slides. | Water simulation in the user's own building |
| Mol, Botzen & Blasch (2022, *Judgment and Decision Making*) | People who experienced a flood in VR invested clearly more in protection than the control group; after 4 weeks the effect had dropped slightly, not significantly. | Readiness findings plus "simulate it" turn into investment decisions (leak sensors, moving servers) |
| Feng, González, Amor, Lovreglio & Cabrera (2018), systematic review of serious games for evacuation; Lovreglio & Kinateder (2020, *Safety Science*), AR for evacuation | Posters, videos and drills are often poorly remembered; immersive games engage more. AR has clear strengths and limits. | Drill playback with people walking out |
| Hagemeier-Klose & Wagner (2009, *NHESS*, TU München) | Many hazard maps are too complicated for lay people. Intuitive maps (blue shades for water depth, comparison with past local floods) draw attention and prompt people to find out more. | Water shown as blue shades by level over time; a calm, readable map |

## How to use this in the pitch (suggested)
1. One sentence per pillar, one number each:
   - **noticed:** 38 % → 77 % with dynamic signs;
   - **remembered:** 3D beats 2D plans;
   - **acted on:** 3D/VR flood experience leads to more protective investment.
2. Then the demo: the same building, an incident, the x-ray view, people walking out, the bottleneck found, the readiness fix.
3. Close with the product line: "**Every incident becomes a drill, and every drill makes the next incident smaller.**"

## Product follow-up (not built yet)
A **training link** per simulation or resolved incident. Employees open it on their phone, see *their own* way out from their room in 3D, walk it once as a guided step-by-step, and the manager sees who has done it. This directly applies Al-Haddad (3D familiarisation), Levine (personal, aligned views) and Zaalberg & Midden (one's own building).
